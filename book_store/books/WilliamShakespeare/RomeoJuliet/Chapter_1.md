**THE PROLOGUE**  
The Chorus outlines the entire tragic arc. Two noble families in Verona, the Montagues and Capulets, are locked in an ancient feud that repeatedly erupts into violence. From these families come two “star-crossed” lovers whose relationship will end in death. Their deaths will ultimately reconcile their families. The Prologue establishes fate, urgency, and the inevitability of tragedy.

***

## Chapter I

### Scene I — A public place

Servants from the Capulet house provoke a brawl with Montague servants. The conflict escalates as Benvolio (a Montague) tries to keep the peace while Tybalt (a Capulet) actively seeks violence. Citizens join in until Prince Escalus intervenes, condemning further disturbances with the threat of death.

Romeo’s emotional state is introduced: he is melancholic and withdrawn, pining for Rosaline, who has rejected him and vowed chastity. His love is presented as immature, idealised, and one-sided. Benvolio advises Romeo to forget Rosaline by looking at other women, setting up the shift in Romeo’s affections.

***

### Scene II — A street

Paris, a nobleman, asks Lord Capulet for Juliet’s hand. Capulet considers Juliet too young and suggests Paris woo her gently. Capulet invites Paris to a masquerade feast that evening.

A Capulet servant, unable to read, asks Romeo and Benvolio to help deliver invitations—unintentionally inviting them. Benvolio persuades Romeo to attend so he can compare Rosaline with other women. Romeo agrees, still focused on Rosaline but foreshadowing his change of heart.

***

### Scene III — A room in Capulet’s house

Lady Capulet informs Juliet of Paris’s proposal, presenting marriage as a desirable and expected step. Juliet responds with dutiful neutrality, agreeing to consider Paris but expressing no strong feelings.

The Nurse, a maternal and talkative figure, provides comic relief and reveals her deep affection for Juliet. Her anecdotal speech emphasises Juliet’s youth while reinforcing her role as Juliet’s confidante. Juliet agrees to observe Paris at the feast, but not to commit.

***

### Scene IV — A street

Romeo, Benvolio, and Mercutio head to the Capulet feast. Mercutio delivers the “Queen Mab” speech, a vivid and chaotic monologue about dreams, suggesting that dreams are products of imagination and can be deceptive or meaningless. This contrasts with Romeo’s romantic idealism.

Romeo reveals a foreboding sense of fate, expressing a premonition that attending the feast will set into motion events leading to his death. Despite this, he proceeds, emphasising the tension between free will and destiny.

***

### Scene V — A hall in Capulet’s house

At the masked ball, Romeo sees Juliet and instantly abandons his love for Rosaline, declaring Juliet unparalleled. Their first conversation forms a shared sonnet, elevating their interaction into sacred, idealised love. They speak in religious imagery, framing each other as pilgrim and saint, and they kiss.

Tybalt recognises Romeo’s voice and is enraged at his presence, wanting to kill him. Capulet restrains Tybalt to avoid disrupting the feast, creating tension and foreshadowing future conflict.

The scene concludes with the crucial revelation: Romeo learns Juliet is a Capulet, and Juliet learns Romeo is a Montague. Both immediately understand that their love is forbidden and dangerous, reinforcing the theme of love constrained by social conflict.

***

**Overall progression of Chapter 1:**

* Establishes the feud and its destructive nature
* Introduces Romeo’s initial immaturity in love
* Positions Juliet as obedient but inexperienced
* Brings the protagonists together through chance and fate
* Marks the shift from superficial affection (Rosaline) to profound, immediate love (Juliet)
* Foreshadows inevitable tragedy and conflict between private desire and public loyalty
