## Chapter 3

### Scene I — A public place

Tybalt confronts Mercutio and Romeo, seeking revenge for Romeo’s presence at the Capulet feast. Romeo refuses to fight Tybalt because he is now secretly married to Juliet, making Tybalt his kinsman. His refusal is misinterpreted as weakness.

Mercutio, angered by Romeo’s submission, fights Tybalt. Romeo attempts to intervene, but this allows Tybalt to fatally wound Mercutio. As he dies, Mercutio curses both families, marking a turning point where private conflict becomes irreversible tragedy.

Enraged and grief-stricken, Romeo abandons restraint and kills Tybalt. Prince Escalus arrives and banishes Romeo from Verona, sparing his life but condemning him to exile. This scene shifts the play from romantic development to violent consequence, establishing the central crisis.

***

### Scene II — A room in Capulet’s house

Juliet anticipates her wedding night with Romeo, unaware of the violent events. The Nurse arrives and delivers fragmented news, initially causing confusion. Juliet learns that Tybalt is dead and Romeo is banished.

Juliet experiences conflicting loyalties: grief for Tybalt and devotion to Romeo. She ultimately chooses Romeo, affirming her commitment to their marriage. The Nurse promises to find Romeo and arrange for him to visit Juliet before leaving Verona. The scene highlights Juliet’s emotional maturity and prioritisation of love over family allegiance.

***

### Scene III — Friar Lawrence’s cell

Romeo reacts to his banishment with despair, considering it worse than death because it separates him from Juliet. Friar Lawrence attempts to calm him, framing exile as a merciful alternative and outlining a plan.

The Nurse arrives and confirms Juliet’s distress, reinforcing Romeo’s emotional turmoil. Friar Lawrence arranges for Romeo to spend the night with Juliet and then flee to Mantua. He proposes a future reconciliation once circumstances improve. This scene reinforces Romeo’s impulsiveness and dependence, contrasted with the Friar’s strategic thinking.

***

### Scene IV — A room in Capulet’s house

Paris meets with Lord Capulet, who interprets Juliet’s grief for Tybalt as a sign of vulnerability. Capulet decides to hasten Juliet’s marriage to Paris, believing it will restore her happiness.

He arranges the wedding for Thursday and instructs Lady Capulet to inform Juliet. This decision accelerates the conflict by imposing a second marriage on Juliet, directly opposing her secret union with Romeo.

***

### Scene V — An open gallery to Juliet’s chamber

Romeo and Juliet spend the night together before dawn forces their separation. They debate whether the bird they hear is a nightingale (night) or a lark (morning), symbolising their desire to delay reality. They ultimately accept that Romeo must leave to avoid execution.

Lady Capulet enters and informs Juliet of the planned marriage to Paris. Juliet refuses, asserting her unwillingness to marry. Capulet reacts with anger, threatening to disown her. The Nurse advises Juliet to agree to marry Paris, prioritising practical survival over loyalty to Romeo.

Juliet rejects the Nurse’s advice and resolves to seek Friar Lawrence’s help. This marks a decisive shift: Juliet becomes isolated, losing familial and maternal support while committing fully to her course of action.

***

**Overall progression of Chapter III:**

* Transforms conflict into irreversible tragedy through death and exile
* Forces Romeo and Juliet into separation despite their marriage
* Intensifies external pressure through the arranged marriage to Paris
* Shows Juliet’s transition from obedience to independent decision-making
* Breaks Juliet’s trust in the Nurse, increasing her isolation
* Establishes the conditions that will drive the final tragic resolution
